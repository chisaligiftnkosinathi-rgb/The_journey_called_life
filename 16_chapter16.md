# CHAPTER 16: PURPOSE

### PART V: BEYOND THE I

---

Walk out into the veld on an autumn afternoon, a kilometer beyond the last fences of the settlement, and you will find an abandoned stone structure.

Its roof of corrugated tin was stripped away years ago. The dry-stone walls, assembled without mortar from grey dolerite boulders, stand waist-high in the tall, dry grass. An old cedar post, bleached by decades of sunlight and split down its grain by frost, leans against an opening that was once a doorway. Inside the enclosure, a rusted iron gear from an old water pump lies embedded in the red dirt, its teeth clogged with gravel and lichen.

A traveller pausing before the ruin immediately asks an instinctive question:

*“What was this built for?”*

The answer is not mysterious. Someone built the enclosure to kraal sheep against jackals. Someone positioned the gate toward the morning sun. Someone hauled the heavy pump gear from the railway siding to draw water from an underground aquifer.

The structure possessed a purpose because an intentional agent designed it to serve a specific, anticipated end. The stones did not assemble themselves to protect livestock; the iron did not forge itself into a gear.

Now, turn your back on the ruin and look at the open hillside.

At your feet, a colony of harvester ants has constructed a mound of excavated soil. Three meters away, a wild acacia tree spreads its umbrella canopy, its sharp white thorns positioned along every branch to deter browsing goats. Overhead, a black-shouldered kite hovers in the thermal updraft, its eyes locked onto the movement of field mice in the grass. A hundred meters below, an ancient seasonal riverbed cuts a winding groove through the sandstone bedrock, carrying runoff toward the valley.

Do these things have a purpose?

The question feels natural. Language nudges us to ask it.

Yet notice what happens to the word *purpose* as soon as we step from the stone kraal onto the open hillside:

The riverbed channels water, but did the sandstone carve itself in order to provide a path for the flood?  
The thorns on the acacia prevent animals from stripping its bark, but did the tree consciously deliberate about defense?  
The heart beating inside your own chest pumps five liters of blood every minute, but did your cells hold a council to decide that circulation was a worthwhile goal?

Here, the word *purpose* begins to split at its seams.

We have arrived at the first fundamental distinction of this inquiry:

> **Function is not automatically purpose.**

---

### Function and Apparent Design

In ordinary human speech, we routinely confuse what something *does* with what something is *for.*

A **function** is an operation within a system. A physical mechanism performs an action that produces regular, predictable consequences.

The heart has a function: it circulates blood through the vascular system, delivering oxygen and nutrients to tissues and removing metabolic waste.  
The eye has a function: it focuses photons onto photoreceptor cells, converting electromagnetic energy into electrochemical signals that the brain processes into a perceptual model.  
The ozone layer has a function: it absorbs much of the Sun's ultraviolet radiation, reducing the amount that reaches Earth's surface.

To say that the heart circulates blood is an empirical observation.

To say that the heart was intentionally created *in order to* circulate blood, or that the universe intended for organisms to see, or that the atmosphere arranged its chemistry so that humans would not burn, is to make an entirely different kind of claim.

For thousands of years, human beings observed the astonishing fit between living organisms and their environments and naturally concluded that intention must be behind it. The wing of the eagle appears designed for soaring; the eye of the leopard appears designed for nocturnal hunting; the human hand appears designed for manipulating tools.

In human experience, many of the complex functional structures people deliberately made arose through conscious manufacture: pots were made by potters, houses by builders, clocks by clockmakers.

It was reasonable, therefore, to assume that living structures must have had a cosmic artisan.

Then, during the nineteenth century, evolutionary biology uncovered an alternative mechanism:

Organisms vary in their inherited traits. Resources are finite. Those variations that confer even a microscopic advantage in survival and reproduction are more likely to be passed on to subsequent generations. Over millions of years, across vast expanses of deep time, this unguided process of natural selection shapes anatomical and physiological structures of breathtaking complexity.

Natural selection produces **apparent purpose** without requiring conscious foresight.

The eye was not engineered in advance by a cosmic draftsman who drew a blueprint and said: *“Let there be vision.”* It developed incrementally through millions of intermediate stages, each of which provided a sufficient biological advantage to be preserved.

This does not disprove the possibility of an ultimate metaphysical creator, nor does it prove that the universe is an accident. Science does not possess the tools to settle ultimate theological propositions.

What it does demonstrate is something more modest and precise:

> **The existence of a biological function does not, by itself, demonstrate a conscious intention.**

---

### The Evolutionary Horizon and Human Meaning

If biology explains how functional structures arise through evolutionary processes, what does it say about the human search for meaning?

Evolutionary psychology and anthropology offer plausible accounts of why human beings possess deep emotional and motivational tendencies. Attachment, parental investment, cooperation, status sensitivity, and curiosity can all be studied partly in terms of the adaptive pressures that shaped our ancestors. But these explanations remain accounts of origins and functions; they do not exhaust the meaning of the experiences themselves.

Yet here we encounter a critical boundary:

> **An explanation of where a desire came from is not an answer to whether that desire deserves to govern your life.**

A human being is not merely an execution engine for evolutionary algorithms.

Consider:

From an evolutionary perspective, traits that contribute to reproductive success are more likely to persist across generations. In that limited biological sense, reproduction is central to evolutionary fitness. But describing that process does not tell us what a human life ought to value.

Yet a human being can decide to remain celibate. A person can choose to devote their life to writing poetry, caring for hospice patients, studying the mathematics of black holes, or tending an abandoned piece of land, with no intention of reproducing.

When someone makes that choice, do we declare that their life was a failure?

Only if we reduce human life to raw biology.

A biological explanation tells us what helped our ancestors survive on the savanna. It cannot tell us what is worth sacrificing for on a Thursday morning.

The organism can investigate its own instincts. It can look at an ancient, hardwired impulse—such as the urge to dominate a rival or hoard surplus food—and say:

*“I understand why this impulse exists in my biology. But it is not what I choose to serve.”*

The question has migrated from the mechanism to the valuation:

Not: *What does my biology drive me to do?*  
But: **What is worthy of my devotion?**

---

### The Question of Cosmic Purpose

And so the human mind turns toward the largest horizon.

It stands beneath the night sky, looking out into the cold expanse of galaxies, and asks:

*Does the universe itself have a purpose?*  
*Is there a grand, cosmic script in which my life occupies an assigned role?*

For millennia, human cultures answered with resounding certainty:

*The stars were placed to tell your fortunes. The gods created humanity to offer praise. History is moving along a predetermined path toward a glorious culmination. You were placed on this earth for a specific, divine reason.*

These narratives provided immense psychological comfort. They assured the fragile human ego that behind the silence of space, a loving author was watching every breath, and that suffering was never in vain because it was part of an overarching plan.

When we examine the universe through our most disciplined empirical instruments, however, what do we find?

Current astronomical estimates suggest that the observable universe contains hundreds of billions of galaxies, with some analyses placing the total in the trillions when very faint galaxies are included. The observable universe spans roughly ninety-three billion light-years in diameter. We find stars forming through gravitational collapse, many spending much of their lives fusing hydrogen into helium, and different stellar deaths ranging from quiet remnants to spectacular supernova explosions. We find planets orbiting silent suns, radiation sweeping through empty voids, and the steady, inexorable increase of entropy across time.

We must state the finding with the strict epistemic discipline established in Chapter 12:

> **We have no established scientific evidence that the universe as a whole possesses a unified intention, a conscious goal, or an overarching purpose.**

The cosmos does not appear to be an arena built to showcase human destiny. As far as observation can demonstrate, the laws of physics operate with majestic, magnificent indifference to whether human beings thrive, suffer, or go extinct.

To admit this is painful. It feels like an eviction from the warm nursery of childhood.

And it is precisely here that many people fall into an intellectual trap.

---

### The Error of Nihilism

When people discover that the universe cannot be shown to have a cosmic purpose, their immediate reaction is often despair:

*“If the universe has no purpose, then nothing matters. Everything is meaningless. Morality is an illusion, love is a chemical trick, and human life is an absurd joke drifting in an uncaring void.”*

This is the philosophy of **nihilism.**

It can sound sophisticated. It can sound unsparingly brave.

But there is a leap hidden inside the conclusion:

> *“Because purpose is not written into the stars, purpose cannot exist anywhere.”*

That does not necessarily follow.

It assumes that the only kind of meaning that counts is **cosmic meaning**—an objective, eternal, universal guarantee stamped onto reality from the outside. If that kind of meaning cannot be demonstrated, it treats every local form of meaning as counterfeit.

Consider how absurd this reasoning becomes when applied to anything else in human experience:

No one looks at a loaf of freshly baked bread on a kitchen table and says: *“Because this bread will not nourish the stars, and because it will decay in four days, it cannot nourish my hungry child today.”*

No one looks at a mother holding her newborn baby and says: *“Because this love will not alter the trajectory of the Andromeda galaxy, it is an illusion.”*

No one looks at a physician setting a broken leg in an emergency clinic and says: *“Because the patient will eventually die decades from now, reducing their pain today is meaningless.”*

Meaning does not have to be cosmic to be real.

A purpose does not need to be inscribed across the fabric of spacetime to possess immense, undeniable validity within the scale of conscious life.

We must resist both the false comfort of cosmic entitlement and the premature conclusion that the absence of demonstrated cosmic purpose makes all human meaning unreal.

The ground between them is where human life actually takes place.

---

### Local and Enacted Purpose

If purpose is not a cosmic object waiting to be unearthed, what is it?

Consider the difference between a pebble and a promise.

If you walk along a dry riverbed, you can pick up a smooth quartz pebble. The pebble exists whether you notice it or not. It has mass, density, and chemical composition; its existence is independent of human consciousness.

Now consider a promise:

A woman looks into the eyes of her partner and says: *“I will stand with you through this illness.”*

Where does that promise exist?

You cannot dig it out of the ground with a spade. You cannot isolate it under a microscope. It is not an eternal physical substance drifting through the vacuum of space.

Does that mean the promise is an illusion?

Not at all. The promise is **real**, but it is real in a different way: it is **an enacted reality.**

It exists because conscious beings, possessing memory, agency, and care, have brought a commitment into existence through their words and sustained it through their actions. If they abandon the commitment, the promise dies. If they honor it across five years of hospital visits and sleepless nights, the promise becomes one of the most powerful, tangible forces in their lives.

Perhaps purpose is of this nature:

> **One way to understand purpose is not as an ancient artifact waiting to be found in the dirt, but as an orientation brought into existence through sustained human commitment.**

Return to Chapter 13:

The gardener did not walk into the winter yard and find a fully grown apple tree hiding under a rock. The tree did not exist until the gardener prepared the soil, planted the seed, watered the roots, and protected the sapling from the wind.

The tree was **enacted** through the gardener’s participation in existing conditions.

In the same way, human meaning is not an announcement delivered by the stars. It is an architecture cultivated in the world:

* When a teacher spends thirty years helping children learn to read, purpose is being enacted.
* When a researcher works through years of failed assays to understand a single cellular receptor, purpose is being enacted.
* When a neighbor carries groceries to an elderly widow who cannot walk, purpose is being enacted.
* When an artist labors over a canvas to render an experience of grief that words cannot reach, purpose is being enacted.

None of these actions require a certificate of cosmic endorsement.

Their value is experienced and evaluated within conscious life: in the alleviation of suffering, the cultivation of understanding, the creation of beauty, and the preservation of life.

---

### The Responsibility of the Open Horizon

To discover that purpose is enacted is both liberating and terrifying.

It is liberating because it frees you from the paralyzing fear that you might "miss" your pre-assigned destiny.

Many people spend decades in anxious agony, wondering: *“What if my true purpose was to be an architect in Vienna, but I became a teacher in Pretoria? Have I ruined my cosmic assignment?”*

We have no established evidence that the universe has assigned you a hidden destiny that you can accidentally ruin. You have not failed to find a hidden treasure chest buried by the universe.

It is terrifying, however, because it strips away the excuse of passivity.

If purpose is not handed to you by the stars, then **no one is coming to give your life meaning for you.**

You cannot sit on the couch waiting for a celestial trumpet to announce what your existence is for.

You are the one who is here. You are the one holding the tools in the garden.

This does not mean that purpose is an arbitrary whim, or that you can simply declare: *“My purpose is to accumulate sports cars and watch television,”* and pretend that such a life possesses deep significance.

Purpose is constrained by reality:

It is constrained by **biology**: Human beings are social organisms, and lives organized around prolonged isolation, domination, or accumulation can carry profound psychological and relational costs.

It is constrained by **context**: you inhabit a specific historical moment, a specific family, a specific community with real wounds, real needs, and real crises that demand a response.

It is constrained by **consequences**: every action ripples outward through the lives of others, generating trust or suspicion, healing or damage.

You do not invent purpose from an empty void. You enact purpose by looking honestly at the world around you and asking:

* *Where is the suffering that I have the capacity to reduce?*
* *Where is the confusion that I have the clarity to illuminate?*
* *Where is the fracture that I have the patience to heal?*
* *What is the work that genuinely needs doing in this small corner of the soil?*

---

### The Shift

And so we witness the great shift of Part V.

In the early stages of this journey, the questions were about origins and selfhood:

*Who made me?* (Part I)  
*How do I see?* (Part II)  
*How do I choose?* (Part III)  
*What is this I that changes and argues with itself?* (Part IV)

Now, the question has turned completely around.

It is no longer:

> *“Why am I here?”*

It is:

> **“What am I going to do with the fact that I am here?”**

I may never discover that the universe has an overarching purpose. I may never find a message inscribed in the background radiation of the cosmos explaining why existence occurred.

Yet I can no longer pretend that my participation in the world is purposeless simply because the universe has not handed me a script.

Perhaps purpose does not need to come from above. Perhaps some forms of purpose rise from within the encounter between an experiencing being and a living world. That is an interpretation, not a discovery about the structure of the cosmos.

And once you realize that your purpose is not an isolated personal project—once you see that your actions, your care, your work, and your breath occur within relationships, environments, and physical conditions far larger than yourself—the horizon widens one final time.

The I is not an island building meaning in a vacuum.

The I is an embedded participant in an immense, interconnected totality.

The question is no longer only what the I was for.

It is what the I belongs to.

We must enter **All That Is.**
