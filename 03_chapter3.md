# CHAPTER 3: THE SOIL

A seed does not choose its soil. It arrives where the conditions of existence place it.
Some seeds fall into rich earth, some into dry ground. Some grow protected from storms; others are shaped by wind from the moment they emerge.
The seed matters. But so does the world that receives it.

A human being is no different. We enter environments that existed before us. A family already has its history. A community has its customs. A language already exists. An economy already has its inequalities.
Before the child can ask, **Who am I?**, the world has already begun answering: **You are this.**

## The first soil
Childhood is where the foundations of the I are constructed. A child does not simply observe the world; the child learns how the world works.
Which voices are safe? What earns approval? What is considered normal?
Much of what we later call *personality* may have roots in adaptations made long before we were capable of explaining them.
A quiet child may have learned that silence is safer. A loud child may have learned that being heard is necessary.
But the seed has its own biology. Two children can grow in the same household and emerge as remarkably different people.
The real question is: **How did the soil participate in creating me?**

## South Africa was already here
Every I enters a particular historical moment.
I was born into a country with a history much older than my awareness of it. South Africa did not begin when I was born.
Its recent history carried enormous divisions. Apartheid had shaped the country before my generation arrived.
The consequences of history remain in geography, in wealth, in education, and in what children imagine to be possible.
A child does not need to understand political history for history to affect the child's life.

## The weather of a household
Soil is not only what is underneath; there is weather.
Some homes are governed by fear, some by affection, some by discipline, some by survival. Most are mixtures.
A household can be loving one day and tense the next. A parent can be both a source of security and a source of conflict.
The I does not grow in a laboratory. It grows among contradictions.

## The people who became soil
People are soil too. Every person who enters a child’s life contributes something—confidence, fear, language, curiosity, trauma, or possibility.
Often they do this without intending to. A child watches adults long before the child understands what they are teaching.
The child observes how people handle pain, how they treat one another, and how they respond to failure.

## The first operating system
Language is the strangest form of soil because we inherit it before we understand it.
Words are already waiting: *Home, Mine, Yours, Good, Bad, Love, Money, Me.*
Does language create the I? Or does an already developing I learn language to describe itself?
Perhaps the relationship goes both ways: the tool changes the user, and the user changes the tool.

## The child and the label
Once language enters the mind, identity becomes powerful. A child is told: *You are clever. You are stubborn. You are shy.*
Eventually, the statement **“They say I am this”** can become **“I am this.”**
A label has become identity.
But identity can also resist the label. This reveals that the environment does not merely give us experiences; it gives us *interpretations* of those experiences.

## What is actually mine?
Is there anything that can honestly be called **mine**?
My temperament may have biological roots. My habits may have environmental roots. My language came from other people. My understanding of success may have been inherited.
Perhaps the answer is not that nothing is mine, but that the I is an **intersection**.
Biology meets environment. History meets circumstance. Language meets perception.
And somewhere inside this intersection, a point of view emerges that says: **I.**

## The dangerous conclusion
If the soil completely determines the tree, how do we explain siblings who become opposites?
The soil matters, but the growing organism is not merely receiving information—it is interpreting it.
Interpretation introduces another layer. The same event can produce different meanings in different people.
The mind does not simply copy the world; it constructs an interpretation of it.

## Before the eye sees
The world enters through the senses. But what arrives in consciousness is not simply the world itself—it is an *experience* of the world.
If the I does not encounter reality directly, but encounters a representation constructed through its senses, brain, and memory, then a new question appears:
**Who is doing the interpreting?**
