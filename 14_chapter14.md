# CHAPTER 14: THE BECOMING

### PART IV: THE I TURNS UPON ITSELF

---

The gardener returns to the apple tree fifteen years later.

The sapling that once arrived in a burlap sack, slender enough to be snapped between thumb and forefinger, is unrecognizable.

The trunk has broadened into thick, furrowed bark. Two lower scaffold limbs were sawn off a decade ago during a severe drought; where the cuts were made, smooth calluses of woundwood have swollen over the scars, sealing the interior against rot. Where a strand of wire fencing once pressed against the trunk, the living cambium has simply enveloped the rusted metal, incorporating it into the solid heart of the wood. The crown is wide, casting a dense pool of shade across the grass that was once bare winter earth.

The gardener touches the rough grey bark.

Almost every visible structure has been modified. The leaves that photosynthesize today are not the leaves that caught the morning light a decade ago; they were formed this spring and will drop into the mulch before winter. Roots have mined three meters into the subsoil, encountering strata of shale the young plant never touched. The fruit hanging from the spurs has a slightly denser tartness than the apples of that first modest harvest.

And yet, without hesitation, the gardener says:

*“This is the tree I planted.”*

We make statements like this every day. We make them about trees, about wooden chairs, about old stone bridges, and about rivers. We speak as though identity were a solid, self-evident fact of the world.

Yet if you stop and press the claim, the confidence begins to waver:

What makes it the same tree?

Not identical material. Not identical shape. Not identical cells. Not identical leaves.

What is the *sameness* we are pointing toward?

---

### The Planks of Theseus

In classical philosophy, this puzzle was given a famous name: **The Ship of Theseus.**

Imagine a wooden vessel belonging to an ancient voyager, preserved in a harbor as a memorial. Over decades of exposure to salt, wind, and tide, the timbers begin to decay. A rotting plank in the hull is removed and replaced by a fresh plank of seasoned oak. A year later, a spar cracks and is replaced. Decade by decade, the shipwrights replace the keel, the mast, the deck beams, and the oars.

Eventually, a day arrives when not a single splinter of the timber that originally sailed from port remains in the ship.

Is it still the same ship?

If you say *yes*, what preserves its identity? It cannot be the material, because every ounce of the original wood has been thrown away. You must be locating identity in something else: in its **structural organization**, its **functional continuity**, its unbroken **causal history**, or the **social agreement** that assigns the old name to the ongoing vessel.

Now complicate the riddle:

Suppose an eccentric harbor master collected every discarded, rotting plank as it was removed, stored them in a dry shed, treated the decayed wood, and eventually reassembled them in the identical design.

Now two ships float side by side in the harbor.

Which one is the Ship of Theseus?

Is it the ship that maintained continuous service, whose timbers were replaced one by one?  
Or is it the ship made of the original matter, reassembled in a dry dock?

The puzzle has provoked debate for centuries because **there is no single physical measurement that can resolve it.**

The universe does not attach an ontological barcode to physical objects declaring their permanent identity. “Sameness” is not a substance you can extract in a test tube. It is a concept whose answer depends, in part, on the criterion we choose to apply.

---

### The Mirror of the Self

The puzzle of the ship is an entertaining intellectual exercise so long as it remains confined to wooden planks and ancient harbors.

It ceases to be an academic game the moment you turn the mirror toward yourself.

Look at your own body:

The skin covering your knuckles today is not the skin that scraped against concrete when you fell as a child. Many epithelial tissues undergo substantial turnover, with cells in some surfaces being shed and replaced over periods ranging from days to weeks. The red blood cells carrying oxygen through your arteries are replaced every four months. Bone tissue is continually remodeled by osteoclasts and osteoblasts, breaking down old mineral matrices and depositing new calcium. Even in tissues where cells persist across an entire lifespan—such as the cerebral cortex and the heart muscle—the molecular components within those cells, the proteins and lipids and receptor channels, are constantly synthesized, degraded, and renewed.

Your physical body is not a monument carved from granite. It is an ongoing metabolism, a localized whirlpool through which water, oxygen, carbon, and nutrients circulate continuously.

Now look at your mind:

The beliefs you held at sixteen—about politics, about love, about success, about fairness, about what mattered in the world—are they identical to the beliefs you hold today?

The vocabulary you use to describe your grief, the fears that wake you in the middle of the night, the jokes that make you laugh, the people whose respect you crave: how much of that interior landscape remains unchanged from twenty years ago?

Even your memories—as we discovered in Chapter 7—are not immutable photographs stored in a private vault; they are reconstructive acts, retrieved into awareness and reshaped by the context of each remembering.

And yet, when you encounter a friend from school whom you have not seen in thirty years, you look across the coffee table into a weathered, unfamiliar face and say:

*“It is so good to see you.”*

And when you look at an old black-and-white photograph of a small child in oversized clothes standing in the dusty yard of Chapter 1, you look at that stranger and say:

*“That was me.”*

What is the thread that bridges the child and the adult?

---

### Memory as a Thread

The most intuitive candidate for personal continuity has always been **memory.**

In the seventeenth century, the philosopher John Locke argued that personal identity extends only as far back as consciousness can reach through memory. According to this view, you are the same person as the child in the photograph because you remember being that child: you recall the sting of the gravel on your knees, the sound of the train on the tracks, the smell of rain hitting the hot corrugated iron roof.

At first glance, this seems obvious. Memory feels like the golden thread running through the labyrinth of time, tying yesterday’s I to today’s I.

Yet as soon as we inspect the thread, it frays.

First, your memory is profoundly incomplete. You retain no episodic recollection whatsoever of your first two years of life—the critical period when your nervous system was laying down the sensory and emotional baselines of your existence. Furthermore, you have forgotten the overwhelming majority of the days you have lived: what did you eat for lunch on the second Tuesday of March seven years ago? What thoughts crossed your mind as you walked to the bus stop on a forgotten morning in your twentieth year? If personal continuity depended upon continuous, uninterrupted recollection, your identity would consist of a few bright islands separated by vast oceans of non-existence.

Second, memory can be distorted. As experimental psychology has repeatedly demonstrated, our recollections can incorporate suggestions, conflate separate events, and project present attitudes backward into the past. If identity were strictly constituted by memory, an inaccurate memory would make you a partially fictitious person.

Third, and most decisively: human beings can suffer severe memory loss while remaining recognizably themselves.

Consider a person who suffers retrograde amnesia following a traumatic brain injury. They may have no recollection of their wedding day, their former career, or where they went to school. Yet their basic temperament, their musical ability, their sense of humor, their capacity to experience love or grief, and their ethical sensitivity may remain clearly evident to everyone who knows them.

Memory is an indispensable contributor to our *narrative* sense of self.

It does not, by itself, constitute the whole of what connects the child to the adult.

---

### The Continuous Organism

If memory cannot bear the entire weight of continuity, what about **the body**?

When a person wakes from general anesthesia, their memory of the intervening hours is entirely absent. Consciousness was temporarily suspended; there was no inner observer watching the clock. Yet they wake in the hospital bed as the same individual in the ordinary sense, while the biological organism has maintained its living organization throughout.

Here, continuity is not defined by static materials, but by an unbroken **causal process.**

The living body is not like a car whose engine can be turned off, disassembled, stored in boxes for twenty years, and reassembled. A living organism cannot pause its existence. It must continually generate energy, regulate its internal temperature, balance its pH, and repair its boundaries against the universal pull of entropy.

In this sense, the body is less like a statue and more like a flame.

A candle flame appears to hold a stable, teardrop shape on a quiet evening. It can be photographed, painted, and identified: *there is the flame.* Yet the matter constituting the flame is completely replaced every fraction of a second as wax vaporizes, combusts with oxygen, and disperses as heat, light, carbon dioxide, and water vapor. The flame is not a permanent object; it is an active thermodynamic event.

Perhaps the physical continuity of the I is of this nature:

> **Identity is not the preservation of an unchanged substance. It is the unbroken coherence of an ongoing metabolic and developmental process.**

The child did not vanish and get replaced by an adult. The child developed into the teenager; the teenager developed into the young adult; the young adult developed into the elder. At every step, the prior state provided the physical and structural conditions for the next.

This is a powerful account of continuity, grounded in what we know about the ongoing organization of living organisms.

Yet it does not entirely resolve the question of first-person experience. A living body can be kept alive on life support long after the cerebral cortex has suffered irreversible damage. The biological process continues; the heartbeat remains; the metabolism churns. But family members standing beside the bed will often weep and whisper:

*“They are no longer there.”*

The continuity of the organism is necessary for human life.

Whether it captures everything we mean by the personal “I” remains an open question.

---

### The Story We Tell

Between the physical continuity of the body and the fragmentary recollections of memory lies another layer of human identity: **the narrative.**

Human beings do not merely experience events; we weave events into stories.

A story provides what raw chronology lacks: coherence, meaning, and trajectory.

We say:

> *“I grew up in a strict household, which made me rebellious as a teenager. Then I experienced a failure that humbled me, which taught me discipline. Later, meeting my partner showed me how to trust, and that is why I am the kind of parent I am today.”*

The narrative acts as an interpretive bridge, linking disconnected fragments of memory across decades into a single dramatic arc.

The unsettling discovery, however, is that **the narrator continually rewrites the book.**

Consider an event from your youth—perhaps a bitter disappointment, such as failing an important examination or being rejected by someone you loved.

At twenty, you told the story as a tragedy, an unmitigated disaster that proved the unfairness of the world.  
At thirty, with some distance, you told it as an obstacle you overcame through sheer perseverance.  
At fifty, looking back across a wider landscape, you might tell it as a fortunate redirection, a necessary door closing that prevented you from heading down a destructive path.

The physical event did not change. The historical facts remained fixed.

What changed was **its location within the narrative.**

And because who you consider yourself to be is profoundly shaped by the story you tell about how you arrived here, an uncomfortable question emerges:

If the story can be revised, reinterpreted, or fundamentally reframed at every stage of life, how much of your identity is an objective discovery, and how much is an ongoing literary invention?

The narrative brings meaning to the I.

It also reminds us that the self can behave like an author, continually rewriting the autobiography through which it understands its own history.

---

### The Fragility of Continuity

To see how complex identity truly is, we must look at the moments where its continuity is disturbed.

In clinical neurology, there are conditions that expose the seams of what we take for granted.

A stroke affecting specific regions of the frontal lobes can produce sudden, radical alterations in personality. A person who was quiet, meticulous, and gentle may become impulsive, disinhibited, and indifferent to the feelings of others. Their memories of their past life may remain intact; their physical body remains recognizable; their legal signature is accepted by the bank.

Yet their spouse may sit across from them in the kitchen, grief-stricken, and confess:

*“The person I married is gone. This is someone else living in their body.”*

In neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s, these dimensions can erode progressively, although the pattern and timing vary between individuals. Recent memories may become difficult to retrieve; familiar places may become harder to navigate; recognition of people, language, and other aspects of cognition can be affected as the disease advances.

Yet even in profound cognitive decline, something often lingers: a sudden smile when a familiar song is played, a soothing response to a gentle hand on the shoulder, a fleeting expression of warmth that pierces the fog.

Where does identity reside when these dimensions diverge?

If the legal system says: *“This is the same citizen,”*  
if medicine says: *“This is the same patient,”*  
if psychology says: *“The personality has fundamentally changed,”*  
and if the individual’s subjective awareness is too disoriented to give an account of itself—

**Who gets to decide what constitutes the person?**

These cases do not demonstrate that identity is an illusion. They demonstrate that identity is not a single, indivisible diamond.

Identity is an overlapping tapestry woven from multiple threads: biological continuity, neurological function, autobiographical memory, emotional resonance, narrative construction, and social recognition.

When all the threads run together, we experience the self as solid and seamless.

When one or more threads snap, the tapestry frays, revealing that what seemed like a single substance was always a complex, maintained coordination.

---

### The Process, Not the Monument

Perhaps our confusion about identity comes from a subtle trap built into language.

In ordinary speech, “I” functions as a noun.

In our grammar, nouns typically refer to stable, bounded things: a table, a stone, a brick, a mountain. We naturally assume that if there is a word called “I,” there must be a corresponding *thing* inside us—a permanent, unmoving core that remains identical from birth to death, like a tiny marble hidden inside a shifting box.

When we look inside, however, we never find the marble.

As the philosopher David Hume famously observed in the eighteenth century, whenever he tried to look most intimately into what he called himself, he always stumbled upon some particular perception or other—of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. He never caught himself without a perception, and never observed anything other than the perception.

When we inspect the brain, we find no marble. We find dynamic networks, rhythmic oscillations, and changing synaptic strengths.

When we inspect the body, we find no marble. We find metabolic exchange, cell division, and developmental unfolding.

This brings us to a profound interpretation:

> **Perhaps the I is not an object that persists through change.**  
> **Perhaps the I is the changing itself.**

Think of a river.

We stand on the bank of the Orange River or the Limpopo. We say: *“This is the Limpopo.”*

Yet the water flowing past our feet is continually being replaced. It fell as rain somewhere upstream, passed through tributaries and soil, and continues downstream toward the ocean. The banks shift with every flood; silt deposits build new islands; droughts reduce the stream to a string of quiet pools.

The river is not an object made of permanent water. The river is an organized pattern of movement, a continuity shaped by geography and gravity.

Could the human I be understood in this way?

Not as an immutable soul or an unyielding substance, but as a living process—a historical, embodied pattern of experience that maintains continuity through the very act of changing?

We must be careful here:

> **To say the I is a process is an interpretation, not an empirical proof.**

Science cannot declare that the self “is” a process, because “the self” is not a material object that can be isolated under a microscope. It is a conceptual framework we use to describe our first-person existence across time.

Yet this interpretation offers a liberating shift:

You do not have to cling to an imaginary, frozen version of yourself to remain real.

You do not have to panic because the beliefs of your twenties no longer fit the realities of your forties.

Stability is not the absence of change. Stability is the coherence of an ongoing trajectory.

---

### The Paradox of Becoming

And so we arrive at the heart of the mystery.

Look down at your hands once more.

Notice the lines across your palms, the faint scars from childhood accidents, the texture of the skin. They are not the hands that held the toy in the dusty yard of Chapter 1. They are not the hands that signed your first contract.

Look at an old photograph of yourself:

You look into those younger eyes. You know things that child could never have imagined: the heartbreaks, the unexpected recoveries, the long journeys, the moments of wonder, the griefs that felt as though they would tear the world apart.

That child did not know you. That child could not foresee this room, this chair, this moment.

And yet, across the vast expanse of years, across billions of heartbeats and trillions of cellular renewals, you reach across the gap and say:

*“I was there.”*

Whatever the connection is, it is strong enough for the present I to claim that distant past as its own.

Is it a permanent metaphysical soul?  
Is it the unbroken causal chain of a living organism?  
Is it the reconstructive power of memory?  
Is it the narrative story we tell to make sense of our days?  
Is it a combination of all of these, woven together into a living tapestry?

**We do not know.**

The mystery remains open, and the open question is more dignified than a closed certainty.

What we do know is this:

The gardener did not create the tree. The gardener participated in its conditions.

And in participating, the gardener was changed.

The child became the adult. The body became another body. The questions climbed to the stars and returned to the earth.

And now, having discovered that the I is not a static monument but an ongoing, unfolding becoming across time, a final, deeper disturbance appears.

If one I can change so profoundly across thirty years…  
If the child, the rebel, the worker, and the elder can all inhabit the same historical trajectory…

**What happens when several different versions of that I seem to exist within the exact same moment?**

Why do you speak in one voice to your parent, another to your employer, another to your child, and another to yourself in the dark?

Why do you want two contradictory things at the same time?

Why does one part of you build, while another part quietly undermines?

The becoming does not merely unfold across time.

It fractures in the present.

We must enter **The Prism.**
