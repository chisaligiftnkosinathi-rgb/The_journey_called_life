# CHAPTER 4: THE FIRST WORLD

Before I could understand the world, I had to experience it.
Before I could name anything, something had to be felt.
Before there was the sentence, **I see the world**, there was simply seeing.

The world arrives before its explanation.

## The first boundary
Somewhere within the continuous stream of early experience, a distinction begins to matter:
Something is happening **to me**.
Something is happening **outside me**.
This is the earliest foundation of the I. Not yet "Who am I?", but "This is happening here."

## The body
I do not experience the world from nowhere. I experience it from a particular body with boundaries: skin, muscles, bones, nerves.
The body creates an extraordinary boundary. On one side, the outside world; on the other, the inside.
Perhaps the biological boundary of the body is the first physical foundation upon which the psychological sense of *me* develops.

## Touch
Touch establishes contact. It tells the organism: **There is something other than me.**
And at the same time: **There is a place from which I experience it.**
The I begins, in part, with boundaries.

## The sound of another
The child is surrounded by patterns. Faces appear. Hands touch. People leave. People return.
The world begins becoming predictable. And prediction is one of the foundations of learning.

## Umwelt: The world as experienced
The term *Umwelt* refers to the portion of reality that an organism can detect and experience through its particular biological capacities.
A bee detects light humans cannot; a dog inhabits a world of chemical information.
There is a difference between **the world that exists** and **the world that an organism experiences.**
The I does not encounter everything. It encounters what its biological apparatus allows it to encounter.

## The eye
We say: *I see the tree.*
The eye is an organ. The retina receives light. The nerves transmit signals.
When we point toward the brain and say, "There is the I," we have not solved the problem. We have located an organ associated with consciousness, but we have not explained why those processes are accompanied by subjective experience.

## Five people and one elephant
In the old story of the blind people and the elephant, each person touches one part and describes a different animal.
This is more than a lesson in perspective. It is a question about the relationship between perception and the I.
Perhaps every human being is touching reality through a limited sensory interface. We experience something real, but we never experience everything.

## The construction of reality
Perception is not simply a camera recording. The brain receives noisy information and constructs a model.
This doesn't mean reality is imaginary; it means the tree I experience is the tree **as encountered by this organism, through this sensory system, at this moment.**

## The strange loop
The I is not sitting inside the brain watching a movie called reality. The I is embedded in a process.
At first: **I experience.**
Later: **I look for.**
Then: **I expect.**
And eventually: **I decide what I believe I am seeing.**

## Before there was "I see"
Before there was "I know," there was experience.
Language gives experience a centre. It allows the organism to say: **This is happening to me.**
And once language creates that distinction, the I becomes explicit.

If the eye is the instrument through which I see, how does the I see itself?
The I looks into experience and finds a reflection.
But is the reflection the I?
Or is it only another image created by the I?
To find out, we must enter the mirror.
