# CHAPTER 9: WHO IS CHOOSING?

You are standing in the aisle of an ordinary room, or paused before an email you are about to send, or sitting at a kitchen table with an employment contract waiting for your signature.

A decision must be made. 

Two futures diverge in front of you. You weigh the consequences. You feel the pull of safety against the gamble of change. You feel a subtle tightness in your chest. You pace the room; you take a sip of water; you deliberate. 

And then, in a quiet, distinct moment, something resolves. 

You say to yourself: *I will take this path.*

You pick up the pen and sign. Or you click the button. Or you speak the sentence that cannot be unsaid.

In that instant, your conviction is complete. You do not feel like a marionette being pulled by strings. You feel like the pilot in the cockpit. The world presented you with alternatives, but it was **you** who reached out, took hold of the wheel, and turned it. You feel responsible for the act. If it succeeds, you will feel satisfied; if it collapses, you will feel regret.

You say: *I chose this.*

Now, stay in that room for a moment longer. 

Do not look at the signed contract or the sent message. 
Look backward into yourself. 

Who was it that just chose?

---

### The Antecedents in the Dark

The moment of decision felt like a beginning—a clean, uncaused point from which a new future was launched.

Yet the instant we begin to trace the lineage of that decision, the feeling of absolute origination begins to fray.

Why did you choose that path and not the other?

You might answer: *“Because it offered more freedom.”* Or: *“Because I have responsibilities to protect.”*

Very well. But why does freedom matter more to you than security right now? Why does that particular kind of risk make your stomach tighten, while another person finds it exhilarating? 

Did you sit down this morning and decide to be the kind of person who values freedom over comfort? Did you author your own appetite for risk? Did you select your metabolic rate, your sensitivity to cortisol, your tolerance for conflict, or the unspoken standards of dignity your family drummed into you when you were seven years old?

By the time the road split in two, the chooser had already been traveling for decades.

The impulse did not arrive naked in an empty chamber. It emerged out of an immense, churning river of antecedents:
* It was shaped by the biological temper you inherited in the dark of the womb.
* It was channeled by the vocabulary of the language you think in.
* It was nudged by what you ate three hours ago, and how well you slept last night.
* It was constrained by the economic realities of the country you inhabit, which made some options visible and others practically impossible.
* It was pulled by the invisible gravity of past wounds—the failure you swore you would never repeat, the person you are still trying to prove wrong.

You can explain *what* you chose. 

What is far harder to explain is where the desire to choose it came from. 

We find ourselves wanting what we want. But can we choose what we want?

---

### The Machinery Beneath the Screen

In the latter half of the twentieth century, neuroscience began pointing its instruments directly at this feeling of volition.

In a series of famous experiments beginning in the 1980s, the physiologist Benjamin Libet asked volunteers to perform a simple task: flex their wrist or press a button whenever they felt the spontaneous urge to do so, while watching a dot rotate on a specialized clock to note the exact instant they became consciously aware of the intention.

Simultaneously, electrodes placed on their scalps recorded electrical activity across the motor cortex.

The result startled the scientific world: a slow electrical change—what neurophysiologists call the *readiness potential*—began building in the brain several hundred milliseconds *before* the participant reported the conscious desire to move.

The immediate, popular conclusion was swift and radical: *Conscious free will is an illusion. The brain decides before you do. Consciousness is merely a spectator watching a movie of its own actions.*

It was a dramatic story. It was also far too tidy.

In the decades since, later work has challenged the interpretation that the readiness potential represents a finalized decision already made by the brain. Alternative models suggest that this early neural activity may reflect background fluctuations of neural noise that merely make an action more likely when they cross a threshold.

More importantly, the entire experiment involved an arbitrary action: moving a finger without reason, context, or consequence. Deciding to flick a wrist while watching a laboratory clock is not how human beings choose careers, make promises, forgive betrayals, or stand against injustice. Complex human decisions are not instantaneous motor spasms; they are slow, deliberate, extended negotiations that take place across hours, months, and years.

Yet even after we clear away the crude interpretations of laboratory experiments, the deeper, more disturbing question refuses to leave the room.

The problem is not simply that "the brain decides before you." 

The problem is: **What do you think you are, if not your brain and body?**

---

### The Pilot and the Cockpit

We tend to speak about ourselves as if there are two separate entities living under our skin:
There is *the brain* (the biological meat, the wet circuitry, the unconscious machinery).
And there is *me* (the conscious thinker, the pure spirit, the pilot sitting behind the eyes).

When science demonstrates that unconscious neural processes precede our conscious awareness, we feel betrayed, as though a stranger has been secretly steering our ship from the engine room.

We ask: *“If my brain started preparing the action before I knew it, was it really me who decided?”*

Notice the assumption hiding inside that question. It assumes that you are only the conscious headline, and that the rest of your nervous system is an alien apparatus.

Why should the unconscious processes of your body be considered "not you"?

When your heart beats, you do not consciously command the left ventricle to contract. When your liver synthesizes glycogen, you do not issue instructions. When your immune system attacks an invading pathogen, you do not direct the lymphocytes. Yet you do not say: *“An intruder is pumping blood; an alien is fighting bacteria.”* You recognize that this vast, self-regulating biological system **is** you.

Why should choice be any different?

The conscious feeling of deciding is not an alien trick played upon you. It is the conscious tip of an immense, integrated organism doing what it evolved to do: navigating a complex world, weighing conflicting motives, and committing its physical body to a course of action.

The unconscious machinery is not your captor. It is the engine that makes choosing possible in the first place.

---

### The Author vs. The Editor

We should discard the myth of the Author—the imagined self that stands completely outside biology, history, circumstance, and causality, creating choices out of nothing by pure, unconditioned will. 

We have no evidence for such a being: a self standing completely outside biology, history, circumstance, and causality, creating choices from nothing by pure, unconditioned will. 

Yet rejecting that picture does not automatically prove that every choice was inevitable. 

There is still a question between those extremes.

If we are not magical authors, are we merely leaves blown about by deterministic winds?

Consider what happens when you write a sentence. 

An impulse arrives. An idea surfaces from the darkness of memory—you did not consciously manufacture the words from scratch; they simply appeared in your mind. That is the biological and environmental momentum at work. 

But once the words appear, something extraordinary occurs. 

Whatever consciousness ultimately is, our experience gives us at least one striking capacity: an impulse can become something we notice, examine, and sometimes refuse to follow.

You can look at what appeared. You can say: *“No, that is not accurate. That is cruel. That is a reaction born of yesterday’s anger.”*

You cross it out. You wait. You allow the system to search for another possibility. 

That capacity is what I mean here by **stewardship**.

The system is capable of examining the conditions that produced the system. 
The instrument can notice its own bias.

---

### The Gardener’s Leverage

This is where the metaphor of our journey deepens.

In Part I, we saw that the seed does not choose its soil. We arrived in conditions we did not design: our biology, our family, the momentum of South Africa's history, the economic contours of our world. We were shaped before we had the capacity to notice we were being shaped.

If the inquiry stopped there, we would be left with fatalism.

But the inquiry does not stop there. Because at some point in human development, the organism discovers that it is not merely a seed buried in earth. 

It can stand up. It can look down at the ground. 
It can become **the Gardener**.

The gardener cannot command the seed to grow into an oak overnight. The gardener cannot change the underlying laws of genetics or abolish the weather. The gardener cannot rewrite the history of the soil or pretend that rocks are not there.

What the gardener can do is clear the stones.
The gardener can redirect a channel of water.
The gardener can notice that a plant is withering in the shade and move it into the sun.
The gardener can decide which weeds to pull and which saplings to protect.

Perhaps choice is not the magical power to act without causes. Perhaps it is the capacity of a conscious organism to become a deliberate cause in its own future.

When you deliberately change what you read, whom you listen to, what habits you repeat, or how you respond to an insult, you are altering the soil. And the altered soil will produce different impulses tomorrow.

You are not free from causality. 
**You are participating in causality.**

---

We began this chapter standing before a diverging road, asking: *Who is choosing?*

We have not found an isolated pilot sitting in an ivory tower behind the eyes. We have found an organism deeply embedded in the physical universe—an organism whose choices are informed by deep biological history, filtered through a constructed perceptual map, and refined through conscious reflection.

You are not the uncaused author of your life. 
You are its **steward**.

And yet...

If choice involves an editor stepping back to observe the impulse...
If stewardship requires watching the habit, noticing the fear, and deciding whether to follow it...

**Then who is the one doing the watching?**

When you catch yourself in the middle of an angry reaction and say: *“I am being defensive,”* who is the “I” that noticed?

The chooser is busy acting in the world. 

**Who is the one observing the chooser?**
