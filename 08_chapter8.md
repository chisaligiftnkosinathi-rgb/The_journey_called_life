# CHAPTER 8: THE OTHER

You are walking down a crowded street. 

The pavement is narrow; people brush past you in both directions. You are lost in your own thoughts, turning over a conversation from earlier in the morning, calculating how much time you have before an appointment, feeling the dull ache in your lower back. 

The world around you feels like a moving backdrop. The buildings, the passing cars, the store windows, the asphalt beneath your boots—they are the scenery through which your life is currently traveling.

Then, you look up. 

A stranger walking toward you catches your eye. 

It lasts less than a second. Your eyes meet theirs, hold for an instant, and release. Neither of you speaks. Neither of you breaks stride. You pass each other and continue on your separate ways.

Yet in that fraction of a second, an extraordinary disturbance occurred. 

Until that moment, you were the solitary viewer of the scene. You were the subject; everything else was the object. 

Then that stranger looked directly at you. 

Instantly, you felt yourself become something visible. You were no longer merely looking outward at the street; you were an object inside that stranger's visual field. They registered your height, your coat, your hurried pace, the fatigue around your eyes. For one fleeting moment, you were an item in a world that did not belong to you.

And then comes the second, far more unsettling realization:

**That person has an inside.**

Behind those strangers’ eyes, an entire universe is occurring. 

They are carrying their own urgent errands, their own private griefs, their own debts, their own childhood memories, their own fear of death. To them, *you* were merely a passing figure on the pavement—a piece of passing scenery in their world.

The solitary theater has collapsed. 
The universe contains more than one center.

---

### The Asymmetry of Pain

Consider the strange, unbridgeable divide that separates one person from another.

When you have a toothache, the pain is not an inference. You do not look in a mirror to deduce that you are hurting. You do not gather evidence, weigh probabilities, or calculate a margin of error. The throbbing ache in your jaw is directly, immediately present to you. It fills your consciousness from the inside.

Now, look at the person sitting across from you at a table.

Suppose they, too, have a toothache. 

How do you know? 

You see their hand rise to cup their jaw. You notice the slight flinch when they swallow hot tea. You see the tight line of their mouth, the furrow in their brow. If you ask, they might tell you: *“It started last night. It feels sharp and relentless.”*

You can believe them completely. You can feel a surge of genuine compassion. You can offer them an analgesic or drive them to a clinic. You have overwhelming, rational evidence that they are in pain.

Yet notice the asymmetry:

**You experience their pain through an encounter with their body, their words, and their behavior.**
**You experience your own pain through the fact of being you.**

You cannot reach into their jaw to feel the throb. You cannot borrow their nervous system for five seconds to measure the intensity of their ache against your own. 

You stand on this side of your skull; they stand on that side of theirs.

This is not a reason to fall into radical skepticism or doubt whether other people are conscious. We have every biological and evolutionary reason to recognize that the human being before us possesses a nervous system fundamentally like our own. 

The point is not that other minds are an illusion. 
The point is that other minds are **epistemically asymmetrical**.

I can know the world of another person through signs, language, empathy, and observation. 
I can never occupy their first-person perspective.

We are separate centers of experience, signaling to each other across an ocean of space.

---

### The Discovery of the Mother's Past

This realization is not born with us. It is discovered, often through a quiet, developmental shock.

In the beginning, a child does not experience its mother as a person with an independent existence. 

The mother is encountered as a constellation of functions: she is the source of milk, the warmth of the blanket, the voice that sings in the dark, the arms that lift when the knee is scraped. She is an extension of the child’s own survival. When she enters the room, comfort arrives; when she leaves, terror rises. 

A young child can experience the mother primarily through her relationship to the child. She is Mother—a permanent fixture of the universe whose presence is woven into the domestic landscape.

And then, somewhere in middle childhood, a crack appears in this comforting architecture.

Perhaps the child finds an old black-and-white photograph in the bottom of a wardrobe. 

In the picture, a young woman is standing on a beach or leaning against the fender of a car, laughing with a group of friends. Her hair is cut in a style the child has never seen. She is wearing a dress the child does not recognize. Her face is radiant, careless, and young.

The child stares at the print with a creeping sense of disorientation.

That woman is Mother. 
Yet the child is nowhere in the picture.

The realization arrives like cold water: 

**She had a life before I was born.**

She had a childhood. She had dreams that were never realized. She had heartaches that had nothing to do with me. She fell in love; she failed exams; she cried in rooms I have never entered; she feared futures that never arrived. 

She existed for decades before I drew my first breath. 

And more disturbing still: **she has an interior world right now that I will never fully see.** 

When she sits quietly at the kitchen table staring out the window, she is not merely "resting." She is thinking thoughts I cannot hear. She is carrying burdens she will never explain to me. She is a solitary human being, facing her own mortality, her own regrets, and her own private mystery.

The mother has ceased to be a function. 
She has become an **Other**.

The child is not the center of the world. 
The child is merely one resident in a world populated by other people who were already living before the child appeared.

---

### The Architecture of Other Minds

Cognitive science has traced the gradual emergence of this understanding.

In early childhood, the mind operates under an intuitive assumption: *What I know is what everyone knows. What I see is what exists.*

If a three-year-old watches a researcher place a piece of chocolate into a blue box, and then sees the chocolate moved to a green box while a puppet named Maxi is out of the room, the child is asked: *“When Maxi comes back, where will he look for the chocolate?”*

Younger children often find these tasks difficult. As children develop, they become increasingly able to distinguish what they know from what another person has seen, believes, or expects. 

The important discovery is not a birthday at which Theory of Mind suddenly switches on. It is the gradual recognition that another person can occupy a perspective that is not mine—and can act according to a belief that I know to be mistaken.

The child discovers that reality is not viewed through a single lens. 

Reality is being encountered simultaneously by millions of nervous systems, each situated in its own body, history, relationships, and position in space and time.

---

### The Gaze That Turns Back

This brings us to the existential core of the encounter.

The French philosopher Jean-Paul Sartre observed that the arrival of another person does not simply add another object to the room. 

Imagine sitting alone in a park, reading a book. You feel comfortable. The grass, the benches, and the trees are arranged around you as things to be seen, enjoyed, or ignored. 

Then, someone walks into the park and sits on a bench opposite you. 

They do not speak. They merely look across the lawn in your direction.

The arrangement of the park is unchanged, but your experience of it is not. The space now contains another center of attention.

Their gaze turns you into an object. 
They look at your face, your hands, your posture, your solitude. They form an impression of you that you cannot supervise. They might find you ridiculous, or pitiable, or intimidating, or entirely insignificant. 

You become an item in a reality you do not author.

Yet the encounter does not stop at objectification. Because as you look back across the lawn, you realize that **they are doing the exact same thing**.

They are an object to you; you are an object to them.
You are a subject to yourself; they are a subject to themselves.

This is *intersubjectivity*: the fragile, miraculous, and often terrifying meeting of two first-person perspectives in a shared physical world.

We cannot fuse our minds into a single organism. We cannot eliminate the wall of bone that separates our skulls. 

We can love, we can argue, we can cooperate, we can build institutions, and we can betray one another. But we can never directly occupy the vantage point from which the other person looks back at us.

---

### The Collision

They look at us. They speak to us. They misunderstand us. They love us, judge us, comfort us, and wound us.

They act in the same world in which we act.

Sometimes their direction meets ours.

Sometimes it does not.

And this creates a new problem.

The world is no longer occupied by one center of experience.

There are other people here, each carrying a perspective I cannot enter, each capable of acting from a reality I cannot fully see.

My actions occur among theirs.

Their actions occur among mine.

We meet at the same crossroads, but we do not arrive there from the same history.

So when two directions meet, and a person must move one way rather than another, another question waits beyond the discovery of the Other:

**Who—or what—is choosing?**

The solitary I has met the world.

Now we must ask: **Who Is Choosing?**
