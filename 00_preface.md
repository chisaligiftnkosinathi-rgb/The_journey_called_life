# PREFACE: THE I

There is a word I have used almost every day of my life.
I learned it before I understood it.
It is one of the smallest words we have, yet it can carry an entire existence.

I.

I am.
I see.
I think.
I remember.
I want.
I fear.
I choose.
I love.
I ask.

But what exactly is the I?
We speak about it as though we already know.
When I say I am tired, who is tired?
When I say I remember, what is it that remembers?
When I say I changed my mind, who changed it?
When I look at an old photograph and say, That is me, what connects the person in the photograph to the person looking at it now?
And when I close my eyes, stop speaking, and become aware of my own thoughts, who is aware?

These questions seem simple until we try to answer them.
This book begins there.

Not with an attempt to tell the story of one person's life, but with a much stranger subject: the existence of the one who experiences a life at all.
My life will appear in these pages, but it is not the subject.
It is evidence.

The experiences we encounter—from childhood and family to education, relationships, loss, ambition, failure, discovery, belief and uncertainty—can become windows through which we examine something larger than ourselves.
Because perhaps my experience is not uniquely mine.
Perhaps the same questions are hidden inside every human life.

Before any of us could say I, an enormous chain of events had already taken place.
There were ancestors.
There was biology.
There was Earth.
There were cells dividing, organisms evolving, generations disappearing and continuing through those that followed.
There was a body developing before there was an awareness of having a body.
There was a world before there was a person capable of saying, this is my world.

And then, somehow, there was experience.
There was perception.
There was memory.
There was language.
There was a developing sense of self.

And eventually, there was a question:
Who am I?

That question may be one of the most remarkable things the universe has produced.
Matter does not merely exist.
At least in us, something made from matter can look at the world and ask what existence is.
Life does not merely continue.
At least in us, life can become curious about its own continuation.
A human being can become aware not only of the world, but of being aware of the world.

And then something even stranger happens.
The I begins to investigate itself.

This book is an attempt to follow that investigation.
I do not want to pretend that every question has an answer.
Where science has something to teach us, we should listen to science.
Where philosophy offers competing possibilities, we should examine them.
Where spirituality offers interpretations of existence, we should distinguish faith from knowledge.
Where evidence ends, we should have the courage to say:
We do not know.

That uncertainty is not a failure of the investigation.
It is part of it.
The purpose here is not to construct a beautiful explanation and then force reality to fit inside it.
It is to observe carefully.
To question what appears obvious.
To examine the things we normally take for granted.

The eye sees, but who is seeing?
The brain processes information, but who experiences the result?
The body changes, yet something calls the changing body me.
Memory changes, personality changes, beliefs change, desires change, cells are replaced, circumstances change—and still the word remains:
I.

Perhaps the I is something stable beneath all change.
Perhaps it is a process continually reconstructing itself.
Perhaps it is an emergent property of a living brain.
Perhaps it is something our current understanding cannot yet explain.
Perhaps the answer is somewhere between these possibilities.

I do not know.
And that is precisely why the question is worth asking.

There is another question beneath it.
If the I is not simply born complete, then how does it become what it becomes?
Perhaps we begin like seeds.
We arrive in conditions we did not choose.
Family.
Language.
Culture.
Geography.
Biology.
History.
Circumstance.

We grow inside a soil that existed before us.
At first, the soil shapes us without our knowledge.
Then, slowly, something changes.
The growing being begins to notice the soil.
It begins to ask why it thinks the way it thinks.
Why it wants what it wants.
Why it fears what it fears.
Why it believes what it believes.

And eventually it may discover something extraordinary:
I can participate in my own becoming.

Perhaps this is one of the great transitions of consciousness.
To move from merely being shaped to becoming aware of being shaped.
From being carried by life to asking where life is carrying us.
From being a seed in the soil to becoming, in some small way, a gardener.

But even the gardener does not control the entire garden.
We inherit conditions we did not choose.
We encounter events we did not plan.
Other people influence us.
Chance interrupts us.
Our bodies impose limits.
The world refuses to obey our intentions.

So the question becomes more difficult:
How much of what I become is chosen by me?
And when I make a choice, how much of that choice was already prepared by everything that came before it?
When I change my mind, who changed it?
When I imagine a future and that imagined future changes what I do today, how can something that does not yet exist influence what exists now?
And if I can observe my own thoughts, am I the thoughts—or am I the one observing them?

These questions lead us deeper.
Eventually, the investigation reaches purpose.
Why does an I ask what it is here for?
Is purpose something discovered or something created?
Does life have a function beyond survival?
Is our desire for meaning merely something evolution produced?
Or is the desire itself evidence of something deeper?

I do not intend to answer these questions on behalf of the reader.
I want to investigate them alongside the reader.
Because there is something unusual about the word I.
Every person can say it.
But no two people occupy exactly the same position from which they say it.
Every I has a different history of perception.
A different body.
A different memory.
A different collection of relationships.
A different environment.
A different sequence of experiences.

Yet beneath all those differences is the same astonishing phenomenon:
There is something it is like to be here.
Something is experiencing.
Something is asking.
Something is saying:
I.

Maybe the deepest journey is therefore not a journey across the world.
Maybe it is a journey toward the one who is experiencing the journey.
This book is that journey.
Not the journey of one particular person, but an inquiry into the strange presence at the centre of every human experience.

The I that was once unaware.
The I that became aware.
The I that was shaped.
The I that began to observe itself.
The I that asks what it is.
And perhaps, eventually, the I that discovers that the question was never as small as it first appeared.

So before we ask what the I is,
let us begin with the most fundamental question of all:
How did there come to be an I capable of asking?
