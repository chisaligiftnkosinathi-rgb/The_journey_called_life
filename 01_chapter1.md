# CHAPTER 1: BEFORE I WAS I

Before I knew my name, there was a person who would one day answer to it.
Before I could recognize my face, there was a face.
Before I could say *my body*, there was a body.
Before I could remember anything, there were already things happening that would become part of the story others would later tell me about myself.

But where was the **I**?

This question is difficult because we normally imagine ourselves as having always been there.
When I look backward through my life, it feels like I am following one continuous thread.
Childhood. School. Friendships. Family. Decisions. Mistakes. Discoveries. Losses. Dreams.
The person I am now seems connected to all of them.

Yet there is a strange problem.
I cannot remember the beginning.
There is no memory of the moment I first became aware.
No recording exists inside me saying: *This is the moment I became I.*

My earliest memories arrive already formed.
I remember something, but I do not remember becoming the one who remembers.
Perhaps this is one of the first mysteries of being human:
**We experience ourselves from the middle.**

We wake up inside a life already in progress.
We do not choose the century into which we are born.
We do not choose our parents.
We do not choose our biological inheritance.
We do not choose the first language spoken around us.
We do not choose the place where our earliest development occurs.
We do not choose the circumstances waiting for us when we arrive.
And yet, eventually, we begin to say: **I.**

How does that happen?

## Before the beginning
Imagine a world in which you do not yet exist.
The Earth is here. The oceans are here. The atmosphere is here.
Plants grow. Animals move. People are born, live and die.
Families carry stories from one generation to another.
History is happening. Technology changes. Languages evolve.
The world does not pause because you have not arrived.

Then, somewhere within this enormous process, conditions come together that eventually make your existence possible.
Biology does not ask for your permission.
Cells divide. Genes are expressed. Organs develop. A nervous system forms. A brain develops. A body grows.
There is no conscious narrator announcing each stage.
And yet, eventually, something appears that can look backward and say: **That was me.**

But was it? Or was it only the beginning of what would eventually become me?
This distinction matters.
An embryo does not appear to possess the fully developed self-concept of an adult.
A newborn does not arrive with a completed biography.
A child does not possess the same understanding of identity as an adult.
The I seems to develop.

But if it develops, then the question becomes: **What exactly is developing?**
A body? A brain? Memory? Language? Self-recognition? A model of the world? A model of oneself?
Or some combination of all of these?
Perhaps the I is not something that simply arrives.
Perhaps it emerges.

## The first person
There is something remarkable about the word *I*.
It does not describe a particular object in the same way that *tree*, *stone*, or *river* does.
If I point at a tree, someone else can point at the same tree.
If I point at a stone, you can pick it up.
But when I say *I*, I am pointing from within my own experience.

You can observe my body. You can study my brain. You can learn my history. You can know my name.
But you cannot directly occupy the exact first-person position from which I experience being me.
And I cannot occupy yours.

This creates an extraordinary separation between us.
We share one world, but we do not experience it from the same centre.
You have your I. I have mine.
A child has one. An adult has one.
A person who has lived for ninety years has one.
A person born without sight can say *I*.
A person who loses sight later in life can still say *I*.
A person whose memories change can still say *I*.
A person whose body changes dramatically can still say *I*.

The contents of experience can change enormously.
Yet the first-person reference remains.
So perhaps we should not ask only: **What is the I made of?**
Perhaps we should also ask: **What makes experience belong to an I?**

## The child who did not know
There is a period of every human life that we cannot personally report.
Our earliest days are largely known through other people.
Someone tells us:
*You did this. You looked like this. You cried when this happened. You loved that. You were afraid of this. You were there.*

We inherit our own beginning from witnesses.
This is strange.
Imagine being given the first chapters of your own life by someone else.
You trust that they happened. You may see photographs. You may hear stories. You may see documents.
But you were not consciously recording the experience as you do now.

The person in those photographs is unquestionably connected to you.
But the connection is not obvious.
The infant has your body. Perhaps your genetic inheritance. Perhaps some early neural foundations.
But does the infant have the same I?
Or does the I gradually acquire itself?

## The name
At some point, someone calls you by a name.
At first, the sound means nothing.
Then you begin to recognize it.
The sound becomes associated with you.
Eventually, if someone asks: *"What is your name?"* you answer.

A remarkable transformation has occurred.
A sound has become an identity.
The name is not the I.
If someone changes my name, I do not suddenly become another person.
If someone forgets my name, I do not disappear.
If someone calls me by the wrong name, I may correct them.

This suggests that identity is deeper than the label attached to it.
Yet the label matters.
It allows the social world to point toward us.
Parents say our name. Teachers say our name. Friends say our name.
Eventually, we say our own name.
The world begins by identifying us for us.
Then we learn to identify ourselves.
Perhaps the I develops partly through this mirror of other people.

## The mirror
Then comes another remarkable discovery.
A human being eventually encounters a reflection and can recognize: **That is me.**
The mirror does not create the person. It reveals an image.
But recognition adds something new.
The body becomes an object of awareness.
The child is not merely experiencing the world.
The child begins to understand that the person inside the world is also part of the world.
There is now a distinction: **I am here. That body is mine. That face belongs to me.**

The I has begun to turn around and look at itself.
This may be one of the great thresholds of consciousness.
The observer has become part of the observed.
I can think about myself. I can judge myself. I can imagine myself. I can change myself. I can ask myself who I am.

## But what came first?
To ask when the I began, we are tempted to search for one precise moment.
Perhaps it was birth. Perhaps the first sensation. Perhaps language.
But perhaps there was never one moment.
Perhaps the I is more like dawn.
There is no single instant when night suddenly becomes day.
Light gradually enters. The boundary shifts.
At some point, we simply say: *It is morning.*

Perhaps the I is like that.
A gradual emergence from unconscious biological processes into sensation, perception, memory, recognition, thought and eventually self-reflection.
If that is true, then the I may not be a thing that suddenly appeared.
It may be a process. A process that became capable of recognizing itself as a process.

## Before I was I
There was a time when I had no concept of my future.
I did not know what I would learn, whom I would meet, or what I would love.
And yet, somehow, the conditions for all of those possibilities were already forming.
Biology had provided possibilities. The environment would provide experiences.
Other people would provide language.
The seed would meet the soil.

And somewhere within that process, the seed would begin to become aware that it was growing.
That may be one of the strangest transitions in existence.
A thing that was once simply becoming begins to ask: **What am I becoming?**

That question changes everything.
Because once the I can ask what it is becoming, it is no longer merely the product of its circumstances.
It becomes an observer of its own formation.

So before we investigate what the I can choose or become, we must first acknowledge the mystery from which all those questions emerge.
There was a time before I could say: **I am.**

**Before I was I, there was everything that made I possible.**
And now the question is: **What was it?**
